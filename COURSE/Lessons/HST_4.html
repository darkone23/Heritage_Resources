Hypertext Sanskrit Tools IV

Complements on Sanskrit syntax

We assume at this point that the reader has read the elementary lesson on the Vikramacarita story in Corpus mode of the Heritage site, and that he/she tried to use the Heritage segmenter on the original sentences, in order to retrieve the analysis provided in the lesson. The lesson actually gives indications on how to understand the tagging, which may be used as heuristics on how to drive the segmentation process. We shall now study a few of these heuristics, and how they relate to the grammar lessons about the syntax of Sanskrit.

Varieties of style in Sanskrit corpus

First of all let us give a warning about Sanskrit corpus. There are many styles of Sanskrit. The sūtra style is very terse. It consists in short aphorisms whose meaning must be studied from commentaries. Typical is the Dhātupāṭha entry for root ‘kṛ’ which we briefly discussed in the terminological note: ḍukṛñkaraṇe (डुकृञ्करणे). This krypic message has to be decoded as P-R-S-M where P is a prefix marker to the root, here ḍu, R is the bare root stem, here kṛ, S is a suffix marker to the root, here ñ, meaning that this root admits ātmane voice, and finally M is the meaning of the root, in ordinary Sanskrit, in the locative, here karaṇe (करणे) ‘in the sense of doing’. This utmost concision of expression of sūtra style is due to the requirement that the whole grammar could be entirely memorized. This is noted with humour in this subhāṣita.

Similarly, Vedic is mostly out of the range of our tools, because of its complex syntax, with floating prepositions not glued to the verbs.

Commentaries use a conventional style to analyse the commented text, both in its grammatical structure and in its interpretation according to a given school. They often use complex argumentation, couched in a technical navyanyāya notation using long compounds, and their understanding demands special training. Here is an example:
रामनिष्ठपुत्रत्वनिरूपितपितृत्ववान् दशरथः.

Litterature uses rarely prose, and most often poetic or epic works use verses, in many meters. For instance, Rāmāyaṇa uses the śloka format, of 4 pādās of 8 syllables. In this style, the punctuation mark daṇḍa (| for the half śloka) is not a syntax delimiter, and merely indicates pause in the recitation (avasāna), and thus absence of sandhi in the text. The double daṇḍa, or pūrṇavirāma (||), separates ślokas, but may not indicate the end of the sentence, which may stretch over two ślokas. Thus, although often a śloka is a self-contained sentence, if may happen that a śloka is composed of two sentences, or even that several ślokas compose a complex sentence. And even the problem of sentence recognition is hard enough to be beyond automatic treatment by computer at present. We give this as warning that one should not always expect that a piece of text constitutes a sentence, even if it corresponds to a poetic unit. However, the segmenter does not care, it will undo sandhi of whatever piece of text in terminal sandhi is given as input, and will respect daṇḍa sandhi isolation.

Ways of paraphrasing sentences

In the course were presented two ways to discuss a sentence grammatically, using a paraphrase in prose syntax (anvaya). Thus daṇḍānvaya explains structurally a sentence by translating it in prose with a canonical order of constituents : first the agent (prathamā), then the complements in their various vibhaktis, finally the verb: Example.

This kind of presentation is appropriate for explaining the sentence, by reordering logically the words so that co-denotating phrases are grouped, and the phrase structure is explicit, on which semantic roles may be assigned unambiguously. A simple sentence is thus explained canonically as S-A*-O-V (subject adverbs object verb), consistently with the S-O-V nature of the language.

However, in real texts, that is outside of commentaries litterature, you will almost never meet the actual sentences in this format. Or if you do, it will rather smack of boring style. Don't expect Kālidāsa to write in daṇḍānvaya style. On the contrary, he will slowly distill padas in the sentence in a mysterious order, and it is only at the end that you will get the crucial last piece of the puzzle, and access the meaning in a flash of insight (sphoṭa). Thus, this method is hardly helpful in reading corpus, and the khaṇḍānvaya must be resorted to. It consists in spotting first the verb, then in recursively filling its successive desires (ākāṅkṣā), thus progressively revealing the kāraka semantic structure. For each kāraka, we spot the head of the phrase, its viśeṣya, then its various qualifiers and adjectives (viśeṣaṇa), which must agree with it. This analytic method may proceed by phrasing questions asking fo the successive desires, i.e. dependencies. This dialectical argumentation is called kathambhūtinī. We call it the Socratic method, in analogy with Socrates teaching his students by asking questions. This is the main heuristic used in the Vikramacarita story.

Now we may relate the two methods. The khaṇḍānvaya method consists in progressively constructing the daṇḍānvaya by reading the sentence pratilomena, from right to left. Thus we capitalize on the general postfix nature of Sanskrit, which applies not only to identifying dependencies issued from semantic roles (kāraka) but also the ones issued from upapadas such as 3-saha, 6-adhas, etc. Also remark that the grammar uses systematically suffixes (pratyaya) for derivation. Sanskrit understanding by reading backwards is similar to the reverse Polish notation used by early computer scientists to evaluate expressions using a stack, and which was used by early desk calculators, where you would compute (5+17)*43 by typing in sequence 5;17;+;43;* each operation taking its arguments in the stack. It may be felt awkward to read backwards a sentence which after all is spoken in a left-to-right manner, but the stack analogy is relevant, when one thinks of the functioning of the short-term memory, where recent cognitions have priority over more remote ones.

Let us show now how this method may be applied to our canonical example दशरथपुत्रः रामः अयोध्यानगरे कोशात् हस्तेन ब्राह्मणाय धनम् ददाति. On the way, we shall use kathambhūtinī by going pratilomena, in reverse gear, with the use of two heuristics, matsyanyāya (मत्स्यन्याय) - The longest segment wins over shorter ones, and bimbasamuddhāra (बिम्बसमुद्धार) - Cherry-picking a relevant lexical item. And of course, we shall use common sense, i.e. yogyatā (semantic compatibility) and sannidhi (proximity), with the provision that poets do not feel bound to these restrictions.

Proper names identification

Cherry-picking happens frequently with proper names, that you may expect to find in the story. There is a difficulty in dealing with proper names in Sanskrit: there is no distinguishing typographical sign like initial capital in English, since the written representation is the faithful encoding of a speech enunciation. Another difficulty is that proper names often denote. Thus telling Kṛṣṇa from the black color adjective is not always easy to decide on grammatical considerations. Some are actually accommodated by grammatical constructions for patronyms, so there are numerous taddhita suffixes that serve this purpose, like son of X, descendant of X, etc. Also, doing simply vṛiddhi of daśaratha, one obtains dāśaratha "issued from Daśaratha", and thus Dāśaratha is a common designation of Rāma, like Vāsudeva is one of Kṛṣṇa. Then many names are actually titles, like bhaṭṭa 'Master', that you must recognize as such, like in kumārilabhaṭṭa 'Master Kumārila'. And of course a commentator of Kumārila will use simply the title bhaṭṭa to refer to him.

But the most common situation is that the name of a person will be a Sanskrit compound that has an ordinary denotation, like Devadatta 'given by God', identical in its structure to French Dieudonné. Many grammatical examples use Devadatta as a dummy, like John Doe, or Alice and Bob for cryptography specialists. Indian philosophers discussing the problem of denotation use the specially coined name Ḍittha (डित्थ) to avoid the problem. But when the name is a compound, it is not always easy to distinguish it as a proper name rather than an epithet, like pītāmbara, who could be Viṣṇu or any person dressed in yellow. Sometimes the compound is not regular, like incurring retroflexion like Rāmāyaṇa, or not dropping the vibhakti (aluk, in grammatical terminology) like Yudhiṣṭhira 'Firm in battle'. But the general case is an ordinary compound, leading to ambiguities.

By the way, what kind of a compound is daśaratha ? It is not a ratha (chariot), so it must be a bahuvrīhi. But it is not just a possessive compound 'he who has 10 chariots'. It does not mean that Dazaratha was possessing 10 chariots, it means is that his chariot span the 10 directions of space, and thus Daśaratha is such a powerful king that he can roam his realm in the 10 directions of space, because he conquered all the earth.

Here is an exemple of difficulty: The sage is called Antilope-horned because he has an antilope horn on his head. We profit of this example to undersand its structure as two elementary sentences liked by tena (therefore), while tasya (of him the sage) qualifies śirasi (his head) and sa (abbreviating nominative saḥ) is the demonstative (he) pointing to its antecedent mahātmanaḥ (this sage). One sees on this example a slick use of pronouns in Sanskrit.

Active, passive, and impersonal phrasings

Let us come back to our simple scheme of sentence S-A*-O-V. This simplistic scheme makes a number of hypotheses. It assumes that there is a finite verbal form in the active voice (kartariprayoga, कर्तरिप्रयोग), that the verb is transitive and thus demands an object in the accusative (dvitīyā, द्वितीया), that the agent is expressed, and thus that there is a subject in the nominative (prathamā, प्रथमा), etc. Actually, the agent may not be expressed explicitly, since the verbal suffix expresses at least its number and person. If the verb is not transitive, it does not need an object. But it may need other arguments, in the dative for verbs of giving, in the ablative for objects of fear, etc. Furthermore, all these actants may be expressed not just as single padas, but as phrases of padas in agreement, and all the padas may be reshuffled in different orders, so the phrases may be discontinuous, and even almost arbitrarily dislocated.

If the sentence is in the passive (karmaṇiprayoga, कर्मणिप्रयोग), it is now the object (karma) that may be expressed in the nominative, and the agent must appear in the instrumental. The question then arises of what it means to be in the passive for an intransitive verb. Then the sentence expresses an impersonal statement (in ekavacana prathamāpuruṣa) like āsyate devadattena (आस्यते देवदत्तेन), which should be translated simply as "Devadatta is sitting", rather than an awkward "It is being seated by Devadatta". Even though this sentence has no subject and no object, it is perfectly acceptable in Sanskrit, as expressing an action and its agent.

Instead of having Devadatta sitting idly, let's have him cook rice. In the active voice, we get devadatta odanaṃ pacyati (देवदत्त ओदनं पचति) and its 8 possible permutations, like pacatyodanaṃ devadattaḥ. In the passive, we get odanaḥ pacyate devadattena and its 6 permutations, where odanaḥ is now in the nominative (prathamā). But odanaṃ pacyate devadattena is also correct, since odana is in ardharcādigaṇaḥ (अर्धर्चादिगणः), which licenses it according to (II,4,31) to be used in masculine as well as in neuter gender, like prototypical ardharca, and odanam is thus a correct nominative form.

But the rice may also cook by itself, in which case Devadatta can get back sitting, and we now get odanaḥ pacyate - rice is cooking. Thus the verb pac has become intransitive, and its object becomes the agent of the action. This usage is explained by sūtra (III,1,87), and is called karmakartā (कर्मकर्ता), 'object acting as agent'. And further beware, if you swap the two padas, it becomes by sandhi pacyata odanaḥ with erasure of "e".

One may wonder why the language has such redundancy that there are 12 ways of saying the same thing, namely that Devadatta is cooking rice. But once you realize that poetry is heavily constrained by meter, you understand that such rich poetry is only possible because of the flexibility of word ordering, that allows to use a permutation of elements to fit the meter. Furthermore, it allows surprise effects and suspense.

Absolute constructions

Moving towards more sophisticated sentence structures than the Simple S-A*-O-V scheme (and its various permutations), we get stories built along the L-Abs*-Simple scheme, where L is an absolute locative clause giving the general circonstance of the episode, which consists of a succession of actions Abs* described by absolutive clauses, and ending in a Simple sentence. These absolutive clauses share the same agent than the main final clause. This agent will often be the subject of this main clause, expressed in the nominative in active voice. But if this main clause is expressed in the passive (karmaṇiprayoga, कर्मणिप्रयोग), its agent will be in the instrumental, and the sentence will be un-translatable directly in English, like in वनाद्ग्रामम् अद्योपेत्यौदनोरामेणापाचि.

Relative clauses

A very common construction of linked sentences is the pair relative/corelative. Typically, the relative pronoun ya or one of its forms or derivate is echoed in the other sentence by the corelative sa. This gives a form of implication, or expresses a causality, often asssociated with quantification: any x is a y, any x such that P(x) verifies Q(x), whenever P we have Q, should x be P, he would be Q (often using the optative लिङ्) etc. Here are a few examples:
यः पठेत् स आप्नुयात् Should you study, you would succeed.
Adverbs of time, space and manner also have relative equivalents:
यथा दृष्टम् तथा लिखितम् Such as you read, you should write (instructions for a scribe).
यः पश्यति न स ब्रूते यः ब्रूते स न पश्यति He who looks does not speak, he who speaks does not see.
Besides the co-reference of the two pronouns, the two connected sentences are independent. Thus the relative and the corelative pronoun, while agreeing in number and gender, may wear a different case (vibhakti), corresponding to the respective role of the common entity in the two sentences.
Sometimes reduplication of the pronouns reinforces the quantification, and additional pairs of adverbs may be combined, as here:
बहुभिः वसुधा दत्ता बहुभिः चानुपालिता यस्य यस्य यदा भूमिस् तस्य तस्य तदा फलम् Many (kings) gave land, and many protected it; whenever anyone has land, he will reap its fruits (land donation title).
